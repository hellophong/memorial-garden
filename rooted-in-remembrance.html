Rooted in Remembrance

There's rosemary, that's for remembrance … and there is pansies, that's for thoughts. Ophelia — Hamlet, Act IV Scene v

A thirty-seven minute ceremony at the front entrance, ending with every person present putting a winter pansy in the ground. The flower is named for the French pensée — a thought. It blooms through January, when almost nothing else here does.

01

The order of ceremony

Five movements. The poem comes before the planting on purpose — so that when people have a pansy in their hands, they already know what it means. Everything is written to be read aloud; times are spoken length, not reading length.

0:003 MIN Executive sponsor or patient advocacy lead

Welcome

Movement one

Delivered standing at the top of the steps, not behind a lectern. No slides, no product mention. The job of this piece is to explain the flower, because the flower explains the day.

Draft — approx. 100 seconds

Thank you for being here.

Every year we set aside time to think about the people our work is meant to serve. Not as data, not as case numbers — as people, with names, who trusted something to us.

Today we're going to put that thought into the ground.

The flower we're planting is the pansy. Its name comes from the French pensée — a thought. Four hundred years ago Shakespeare had Ophelia hand them out saying "there's pansies, that's for thoughts," and the flower has carried that meaning ever since. It isn't an accident that we chose it.

It's also, practically, a flower that blooms in the cold. These will be out through January, when there is nothing else in bloom on this campus. That felt right. Remembrance shouldn't only happen when it's easy.

In a few minutes each of you will plant one. They'll be here all winter, they'll be at their best in March, and every time you come through this entrance between now and then you'll walk past something you put there.

Before we plant, we're going to listen. First to a poem, then to some music.

0:033 MIN Employee volunteer — not an executive

Poetry reading

Movement two

Read slowly, once, without introduction — the welcome has already set it up. A colleague reading beats a leader reading; it keeps the day from feeling staged.

Remember
Christina Rossetti, 1862 · public domain
Remember me when I am gone away, Gone far away into the silent land; When you can no more hold me by the hand, Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay. Remember me when no more day by day You tell me of our future that you plann'd: Only remember me; you understand It will be late to counsel then or pray. Yet if you should forget me for a while And afterwards remember, do not grieve: For if the darkness and corruption leave A vestige of the thoughts that once I had, Better by far you should forget and smile Than that you should remember and be sad.

Line twelve is why this is the pick: a vestige of the thoughts that once I had. The poem lands on the same word the flower is named for, and it ends by releasing the room rather than holding it in grief — which is the right note before people go and do something with their hands.

  • Whitman — Song of Myself, §6"the beautiful uncut hair of graves" Public domain Warmer and more expansive, less elegiac. Choose this if the tone should lift rather than settle. Slightly long — trim to twelve lines.
  • Dickinson — The Bustle in a HouseEight lines, about ninety seconds Public domain The shortest option and the most severe. Powerful, but it leaves the room heavier than Rossetti does.
  • Mary Oliver or Wendell BerryFrequently requested for this kind of event Permission needed Both are still in copyright. Reading aloud at a company event needs written permission from the publisher — allow four to six weeks, and have a public-domain backup approved in case it's declined.
0:066 MIN Solo cellist — hired, or from staff

Music performance

Movement three

One formal piece, seated and still. Then — and this is the part worth insisting on — the same musician keeps playing quietly underneath the planting. Forty people digging in silence is uncomfortable; forty people digging to a cello is a ceremony. Book the player for the full block, not just the solo.

  • Bach — Cello Suite No. 1 in G, PréludeSolo cello · 2:30 Recommended Universally recognised without being sentimental, and it carries outdoors better than almost anything else. Follow with the Sarabande underneath the planting.
  • ShenandoahVoice or solo instrument · 3:00 Traditional The local choice — an American river song for a Virginia campus. Sung unaccompanied it will stop the room. Best if you have a genuine singer on staff.
  • Contemporary songAnything under copyright Licensing needed Live performance of copyrighted music at a company event needs a performance licence unless the property already carries a blanket one. Public-domain and traditional repertoire sidesteps the question entirely.
0:1218 MIN Facilitator + 3 garden marshals

The planting

Movement four — the reason everyone is here

Three minutes of instruction, fifteen of planting and watering. Plants are already set out in position on the soil, so nobody has to make a layout decision — they lift a pot, plant it where it stood, and firm it in. Music continues throughout. Marshals hand out tools, keep the beds moving, and quietly fix anything planted proud.

Facilitator — what to say before they start

There are about two hundred and ninety plants and roughly sixty of us, so take five each. They're already sitting where they need to go — just lift the pot, dig where it was standing, and put it back in.

Deep enough that the top of the root ball is level with the soil. Firm it down with your thumbs — pansies like to be tucked in tight. Then water it, which is the last thing we'll do together.

The planters are waist height, so there's no kneeling unless you want to. Take your time. We're not in a hurry.

0:334 MIN A different voice from the welcome

Closing statement

Movement five

Given standing beside the planted bed, not back at the steps. Ideally somebody closer to patients than to the org chart — medical affairs, patient services, a nurse educator. Then no dismissal: people should be allowed to drift.

Draft — approx. 75 seconds

That's the last of them in.

What you've just done took about fifteen minutes and will last about six months. They look small today. They always do. Give them until March.

I want to say one thing about why we do this outside, with our hands, instead of in a conference room with a slide.

It's easy to talk about patients. It's harder to do something slow and physical and slightly inconvenient in their memory — and that difficulty is the point. We wanted the day to cost us something small.

The garden will be here. It doesn't need anything from you except that you notice it on your way in.

If you'd like to stay a few minutes, please do — there's no next thing. And if you'd like to plant again next year, we'll be right here. Same beds, same flower.

Thank you for your time, and thank you for your care.

03

Making the planting actually work

This is the segment that fails if nobody plans it. Sixty untrained people, fifteen minutes, and a bed that has to look deliberate afterwards.

The day before

Crew preps, nobody digs

Strip the spent summer material, top up with fresh planter mix, water thoroughly, and let it settle overnight. Wet soil on the morning of is heavy, muddy and cold on the hands.

  • Fresh mix to within 2 in. of the cap
  • Slow-release feed worked in
  • Water the night before, not the morning of
Ninety minutes before

Set every pot in its final spot

The single highest-leverage thing on this list. Crew places all 290 pots, still potted, at 6–7 in. centres in the exact layout. Participants never make a spacing decision, so the bed reads as designed instead of as a crowd.

  • 6–7 in. centres, staggered rows
  • Colour drifts pre-sorted by the crew
  • Marshals keep spare plants out of sight
Kit

Enough tools, not too many

One trowel between two people keeps it social and stops the beds from getting crowded. Gloves offered, never required — a lot of people will want to feel the soil.

  • 30 hand trowels, 60 pairs of gloves
  • 6 watering cans, filled and staged
  • Hand-wash station and towels at the exit
  • Bin for empty cell packs, out of sightlines
Access

The raised planters are a gift

All three zones sit at roughly waist height, so a wheelchair user or anyone who can't kneel participates on exactly the same terms as everyone else. That is unusual and worth saying out loud in the facilitator's instructions.

  • Keep a clear 4 ft approach at the entry planter
  • Reserve the lowest-reach run for anyone seated
  • Two folding stools available, unremarked
Flow

Split the group across three zones

Sixty people at one 22 sq ft planter is a scrum. Marshals walk thirds of the group to the wedge and the wall strip as the music starts, so all three zones plant simultaneously.

  • Zone 1 entry planter — 20 people, 80 plants
  • Zone 2 the wedge — 20 people, 105 plants
  • Zone 3 wall strip — 20 people, 105 plants
Weather

It goes ahead in the rain

Light rain is fine and arguably better — the soil is soft and the watering step takes care of itself. Have the call ready for anything worse.

  • Umbrella stand and 40 spares at the door
  • Cello does not play in rain — have a recording and a speaker
  • Hard-weather call by 7am, notice by 8am
  • Rain date exactly one week later, same time

Spacing

6–7 inches on centre

Closer than a nursery will tell you. Pansies are annuals here — they never need room to grow into, and a tight planting looks finished on the day rather than in six weeks.

Timing

Mid-October, soil still warm

Roots need soil around 60°F to establish before hard frost. Planted mid-October they bloom through mild winter spells, hold through January, and peak in late March.

Aftercare

Deadhead in February

One pass pulling spent flowers in late February roughly doubles the March display. Assign it to a named person now — this is the step that always gets dropped.

06

Four things to settle early

  • Deer will eat these

    Pansies are among the most heavily browsed bedding plants there is, and pressure on this site is high. Budget for repellent applied the morning after planting and re-applied every three to four weeks and after heavy rain. Don't spray during the ceremony — it smells.

  • The date is load-bearing

    Winter pansies only work planted in autumn. If Patient Appreciation Month falls in spring, these go in and fry by June, and the whole plant choice needs revisiting. Confirm the month before ordering anything.

  • Pansies are annuals

    They'll be pulled next autumn. That suits an annual ceremony — the replanting is the ritual, and it repeats cleanly every year. If you also want something permanent to point at, the ground bed beyond the wall is still available for a tree in a later year.

  • Keep it non-promotional

    No product or brand marks on signage or in the garden. Patient names or images need written consent on file, and any dedication wording is worth routing past legal and medical review. If patients or families attend, brief staff that the day involves no product discussion, and have photography releases at check-in.
